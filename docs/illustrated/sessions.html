Sessions and fencing

A network partition looks exactly like a dead client. EFS therefore never takes a client's locks or open files on a timeout alone. It fences the old session with a real barrier first, and only then reclaims what it held.

The session

Every mount has one

A session is a client identity plus an epoch, committed through Raft. Every change the client sends carries the identity, the epoch and a request id.

Stale epochs are refused

Once a newer epoch is committed, a request from the old one is rejected as stale. Stale work is harmless, never accepted.

Retries are safe

The request id lets a server recognise a repeated request and answer the recorded result instead of doing it twice, even after a lost reply.

Storage nodes check too

Before a fragment PUT, the storage node asks the metadata leader whether the upload ticket is still admitted. A fenced client's PUT is refused. Orphans come from a writer that stored fragments but never published.

Revoking a session: a barrier, not an announcement

Committing the new epoch is not enough: other shards may still act on the old one. So revocation is a ladder, and nothing that depends on the old session being dead happens before its last rung. This is the sequence a clean unmount runs. Choose how many of the three storage members are down.

    • Majority-sealed fence. The storage fence seals every member that answers and needs a strict majority. Sealing makes a member refuse any PUT from the old epoch. With one member down the unmount completes; with two down it fails closed and reports phase=storage-fence, and nothing is reclaimed.
    • A returning member catches up first. A member that missed the fence learns the committed epoch before it accepts a PUT or deletes an orphan, so it can never serve the old epoch.
    • The client routes around a dead member. If a hint names a dead voter, the revoke is retried through another one.

    What a client stop prints

    client.sh stop refuses while a process uses the mount, then follows the client's stop record through its phases and waits while a phase or step changes at least every 30 s, with a 600 s cap on the whole wait.

    A stall or failure is reported with its reason and the client is not killed. --force-discard detaches and loses unresolved writes, for a client that cannot drain. A signal or plain umount bypasses the drain.

    POSIX locks and a crashed client

    Lock records live on the metadata shard of the inode, so every client sees one authority. A blocked waiter waits there. Try the sequence: a holder dies, but the lock stays until the session is fenced.

    • Range arithmetic is plain POSIX: ranges split, merge and upgrade as fcntl specifies, all decided by the one authority.
    • Deadlock detection covers cycles through one inode, which is all POSIX requires for a single-lock wait.
    • Heartbeat loss only starts the attempt to replace a session. The fence decides.

    Open but unlinked

    If a file is unlinked while someone has it open, the name goes at once, but the inode and its data stay until the last descriptor closes. The open is recorded as a lease under the session. If the client dies, fencing drops the lease and the orphan becomes reclaimable. That is the same road the space page follows.

    When a client dies

    What the client hadWhat happens
    Writes it had published (fsync, close)Durable and visible. Nothing to do.
    Buffered writes it had not publishedLost. This is the POSIX contract of a buffered write, not a failure.
    Fragments uploaded but never publishedOrphans, collected later; status counts abandoned uploads.
    Locks and open-unlinked leasesReclaimed after the revocation barrier completes, not before.
    Append reservations in flightResolved as zero-filled holes so later appends are not blocked.
    Retries still in flightRecognised as duplicates; the recorded outcome is returned.
